Abstract
Long duration microgravity experiments aboard the International Space Station determine diffusion flame
extinction limits of PMMA spheres. Upon ignition from an electrically-heated coil, the 4-cm-diameter samples are
exposed to forced flows ranging from 0.2 to 80 cm/s and an oxygen ranging from 13 to 28% in one atmosphere
total pressure. Extinction is reached as oxygen concentration gradually decreases by natural depletion. Five
extinction tests are presented at different flow velocities and oxygen concentrations. Quenching at low velocity is
observed with the flame tip shrinking upstream and blowoff is observed when a hole forms in the flame at the
forward flow st agnation point. However, these processes are not quasistatic. The quenching motion involves
periodic flame tip pulsating toward downstream and shrinking upstream with a continually decreasing flame size
at the end of each cycle. In blowoff, the flame base pulsates between the flame hole and the downstream location,
although with far fewer cycles compared to quenching. The pulsations appear to be the result of a premixed flame
front spreading into a combustible mixture. Two specific cases are discussed. In the first, at very low flow velocity
(~ 0.4 cm/s) and elevated oxygen, self-sustained flame tip cyclic pulsations are observed for a lengthy period (~15
minutes). In the second case with a higher flow velocity (50 cm/s), the diffusion flame is stabilized at the shoulder
of the spherical sample after local stagnation point blowoff. With steady decrease in ambient oxygen due to
depletion, spinning flamelets are formed. The long-duration microgravity environment makes it possible to observe
these interesting and detailed extinction processes.
Keywords: Flame quenching; Flame blowoff; Flame pulsation; Low stretch flame; Microgravity

1. Introduction 1
Extinction of diffusion flames is a classical topic 2
with practical relevance to many combustion systems. 3
Aerodynamic blowoff due to flow residence times can 4
be interpreted using a critical small Damkohl er 5
number concept [1,2] and is a flame stabilization 6
problem. Aerodynamic quenching in slow flow (at 7
long residence times) is the consequence of flame heat 8
losses [3]. In slow flows, the combustion intensity is 9
reduced and the ratio of heat loss (by radiat ion, for 10
example) to heat generation is increased. The 11
resulting reduced flame temperature leads to 12
quenched extinction. Although blowoff experiments 13
are common, aerodynamic quenching studies in an 14
open-air environment (long residence time) are 15
uncommon, e specially for solid fuels [4,5]. This is 16
because the magnitude of the flow required for 17
quenching is typically lower than that of the always 18
present buoyancy -induced flow in normal Earth 19
gravity. On Earth, low buoyant stretch stagnation -20
point flames can on ly be obtained using very large 21
diameter solid samples [6] or large radius burners 22
[7,8]. To test a sample of a given size in low -speed 23
flow, a microgravity environment is needed.  In 24
microgravity, the magnitude of the imposed (forced) 25
flow can be independently controlled. 26
Microgravity experiments are also important to 27
spacecraft fire safety. Understanding extinction limits 28
of solid materials is important to interpret the results 29
of material screening tests performed on earth [9,10]. 30
For thick solids with large solid -phase thermal 31
response times, long -duration microgravity 32
environments are needed. The present work is a part 33
of the Growth and Extinction Experiment (GEL), 34
which is the first of the five original experiments in 35
the SoFIE ( Solid Fuel Ignition and Extinction) 36
project. SoFIE is a NASA Biological and Physical 37
Sciences (BPS) sponsored project on the International 38
Space Station (ISS) on solid combustion [11]. The 39
solid sample used in GEL is a 4-cm diameter PMMA 40
(polymethylmethacrylate) sphere. The experimental 41
findings of both quenching and blowoff processes are 42
reported here, emphasizing the differences of their 43
near-limit behaviors.  44

2. Experiment setup  46
47
The experiment is conducted using the SoFIE 48
insert (main components shown in  Fig.1) which is 49
placed inside the Combustion Integrated Rig (CIR) 50
aboard the ISS. CIR is equipped with a power supply, 51
filters, lighting, gas cylinder intakes, radiometers, and 52
oxygen, pressure, velocity and CO, CO 2 sensors. In 53
this paper, oxygen percenta ge is reported from the 54
sensor located at the flow duct entrance. The SoFIE 55
insert has a square flow duct (13.3 cm x 13.3 cm cross-56
section and 24.6 cm long). Forced flow ranging from 57
0.2 - 80 cm/s is generated by two fan banks. A flow 58
sensor at the upstream end of the flow duct provides a 59
flow velocity reading. The spherical PMMA sample 60
(4 cm diameter) used in GEL is supported by a long 61
stainless-steel tube (6.35 mm diameter). Three 62
samples are mounted 120  apart on an index system 63
that can be rotated to install one sample at a time with 64
the flow duct.  65
Once the astronaut installs the samples and the 66
oxidizer gas cylinders in the ISS, the rest of the 67
experiment is controlled remotely from the NASA 68
Glenn Research Center ISS Payload Operations 69
Center (GIPOC). During the test, one sample is placed 70
along the centerline of flow duct and about 1 cm from 71
the duct exit for ignition. The igniter is an electrically 72
heated coiled wire placed near the upstream 73
stagnation point of the sphere. After ignition, the 74
igniter is retracted. The sample can either stay at this 75
location for the whole test or be translated into the 76
duct.  77
Four fixed cameras are used for flame 78
visualization. Three Sony FCB-MA130 cameras with 79
a 1400 x 1200 pixel array (named SoFIE Cam1,Cam2, 80
and Cam3) are in orthogonal direction of a 1.4 81

camera assembly with a 1360 x 1024 pixel array. 83
SoFIE Cam1 views the sample near the ignition 84
location. SoFIE Cam2 and ACME are aligned and 85
view the sample at the location af ter translating into 86
the duct. SoFIE Cam3 is not activated in the cases 87
presented in this paper.  88
All the extinction cases in this paper are obtained 89
using a natural and gradual oxygen depletion while 90

limits and for observing spontaneous cyclical 96

oxygen concentration measurement is made at the 98

3.3 Extinction at intermediate flow velocities 7
and flow strain rates  8
9
In the previous sections we have shown how 10
different the extinction processes for low -flow 11
quenching and high -flow blowoff are. In quenching, 12
the flame tip shrinks toward the upstream, in blowoff, 13
the flame base retreats toward the downstream. In 14
quenching, the flame tip pulsates while in blowoff, the 15
flame base pulsates. One natural question is how does 16
the flame go to extinction at intermediate flow 17
velocities where the quenching and the blowoff 18
boundaries meet? The meeting point of the two 19
branches has been referred to as the fundamental limit 20
where the oxygen limit is the lowest [3].  21
We have examined several test results in the 22
intermediate flow range between 9.22 and 11.5 cm/s 23
(strain rates 6.92 -8.23 s-1). They all exhibit the same 24
description on the extinction processes as follows. As 25
oxygen is depleted, flame tip shrinks but without any 26
pulsation. Near extinction, the flame base color began 27
to fade (a prelude to local blowoff or hole forming) 28
but the fl ame goes out normally in one video frame 29
without any base pulsation either. So, the transition 30
from quench to blowoff is smooth along the extinction 31
boundary. Case #3 in Table 1 is a representative 32
intermediate case (see supplementary video S3).   33

3.4 Self-sustained cyclic flame pulsation 35
36
Case #4 is an ultra -low velocity case at relatively 37
high oxygen atmosphere. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the 38
flame tip pulsating downstream starts at 311 s, 39
immediately after the nominal flow velocity is turned 40
down to 0.4 cm/s at 309 s, and lasts until quenching at 41
1237.88 s, for a total period of 927 s. In contrast, the 42
near-limit pulsation in Case #1 only lasts for 18.84 s. 43
In addition to flame tip position, test telemetries of 44
oxygen, pressure, and flow velocity are also plotted in 45
Fig. 5(a). It shows one side of the flame initiates the 46
cyclic pulsation and the other side starts much later at 47
around 1040 s. When the pulsation starts, oxygen 48
concentration is at 26.25%. It gradually depletes to 49
20.77% at quenched extinction . During most of the 50
time, the pulsations are under elevated oxygen 51
concentration. If the oxygen were not depleted, the 52
pulsative cycles would have lasted even longer. So, 53
we will refer this case as a “self -sustained” flame 54
pulsation (i.e., limit cycle). During pulsations, though 55
the nominal flow velocity is held constant at 0.2 cm/s 56
(except the 0.4 cm/s set flow in the beginning 10 s), 57
the flow sensor reading ranged from 0.16 to 0.5 cm/s. 58
Similar to Case #1, there is a gradual increase of flow 59
velocity dur ing the quenching process (Fig. 5(a)). 60
Starting at around 880 s, there is also flow velocity 61
oscillations in phase with the flame pulsation. Figure 62
5(b) shows 10 s of the flame tip tracking and the flow 63
sensor reading near extinction . The flow velocity 64
fluctuation is thought to be the result of flame -flow 65
interaction since the flow is very weak and these 66

in the duct.  68

4. Summary and discussions 46
47
This is the first report on the experimental findings 48
from a recent microgravity experiment aboard the 49
International Space Station studying the burning of a 50
4-cm diameter PMMA sphere. One of the main 51
objectives of the experiment is to study solid diffusion 52
flame extinction processes. Using an oxygen 53
depletion method at specified flow velocities, both the 54
transient details of the low-flow quenching and high-55
flow blowoff processes are revealed.  56
Quenching starts with the flame tip shrinking 57
toward the stagnation region. Extinction occurs when 58
the shrinking flame at the forward stagnation point 59
becomes too small. An early quasi-steady model over 60
a thin solid with surface radiative heat loss has 61
predicted this trend [21]. The present experiment, 62
however, shows t hat this trend of steady shrinking 63
flame is only true for the average flame. The actual 64
quenching process is very dynamic involving flame 65
tip local fluctuation and downstream cyclic pulsations 66
all the way to the back of the sphere over many cycles. 67
In puls ations, flame tip spreads into partially 68
premixed mixture next to the hot solid surface. The 69
mixture is created by leakage of fuel vapor from the 70
opening between the flame tip and the solid surface 71
and solid pyrolysis from the still hot solid after the 72
flame tip cyclic shrinking. In low stretch flames, this 73
opening is large, which enhances the importance of 74
fuel leakage contribution to the cyclic pulsations. In a 75
higher oxygen atmosphere (>21%) and ultra-low flow 76
velocity (< 1 cm/s), pulsative motion can last for more 77
than 15 minutes. These implications for spacecraft 78
safety need to be explored further.  79
Blowoff starts with a hole opening at the stagnation 80
region in front of the envelope diffusion flame. This 81
is well known experimentally and studied in detai l in 82
[20]. An early quasistatic numerical model on thin 83
solid also predicted the local blowoff of the stagnation 84
flame and the possibility of side stabilized diffusion 85
flame with increasing flow velocity or decreasing 86
Damkohler number [22]. Again, experime nts show 87
that the extinction process is dynamic. Cyclic 88
upstream pulsation and flame base retreat can proceed 89
to the stabilization of the flame at the shoulder of the 90
spherical sample. When the flame is weakened by 91
reduced oxygen, flamelets form and azimuthally spin 92
around the sphere. Other possible wake flame 93
configurations have not been observed in the present 94
setup possibly owing to the presence of the sample 95
supporting rod.  96
Regarding the near -limit flame extinction 97
dynamics: while cyclic flame movement are observed 98
both in quenching and in blowoff, there is a profound 99
difference between the two cases. The pulsating part 100
is the flame tip in quenching and the flame base in 101
blowoff. The stagnation region is the flame 102
stabilization zone whereas tip is not. However, both 103

We note that blowoff and quench have been 3

The multiple embedded thermocouples enable us to 7

Also, regarding the scale effect of the samp le on 11
quenching: low stretch flames can be established 12
using large samples in higher velocity flows [6 -8] or 13
using smaller sample in lower velocity flow such as in 14
the present work. Although both can achieve the same 15
low flame stretch rate, the near -limit behavior can 16
differ in the details.  In large sample, flame breaks into 17
flamelets, strips or holes before the quenching of the 18
complete flame. The flamelets are of the dimension of 19
diffusion length. In the present work using smaller 20
sample, there is pulsat ive motion but no flamelet 21
formation during the quenching process. So, the 22
detailed quenching and blowoff process can be multi-23
dimensional in nature. They cannot be prescribed by 24
the flow strain or flame stretch rate alone. An 25
additional length scale may be required.  26
One final remark on the diffusion flame extinction 27
criterion.  Even in the one-dimensional description of 28
diffusion flames, one may wonder whether a single 29
Damkohler number can be sufficient to describe both 30
blowoff and quenching limits. Both are extinction 31
processes and the concept of limiting ratio of chemical 32
vs. flow times seems applicable. However, 33
quantifying the chemical reaction rate will need 34
accurate flame temperature information. The actual 35
flame temperature will be affected by heat loss, so 36
additional non -dimensional heat loss parameter(s) 37
will be required. We believe that quenching and 38
blowoff are distinct flame extinction modes having 39
different characteristics. Blowoff at high speed is due 40
to short flow residence time in the flame stabilization 41
zone. Blowoff can occur even at adiabatic flame 42
temperature (i.e., no heat loss) as demonstrated in 43
classical theory. So, although heat loss may modify 44
the blowoff boundary, its effect is modest (ratio of 45
heat loss to heat generation is small ) and the flame 46
temperature drop below the adiabatic value can be 47
relatively small. On the other hand, at low stretch (low 48
flow speed) with long flow residence time (or large 49
flame standoff distance), aerodynamic quenching 50
occurs when heat loss becomes a s ignificant fraction 51
of the total heat release of the flame. This causes 52
substantial flame temperature reduction and leads to 53
extinction. We have demonstrated in the GEL 54
experiment not only the existence of the two 55
extinction branches but also describe thei r different 56
mechanisms and transient responses. In addition, the 57
flame behaviors when the two extinction boundaries 58
merge have been clarified. 59
