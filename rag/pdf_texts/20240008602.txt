Vehicle Modeling during the Burning of Thermally Thick
Samples in the Saffire V Experiments
Justin Niehaus1, John Brooker2, David Urban3, Gary Ruff4
NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 44135, USA
In order to design a safer spacecraft in terms of fire prevention, detection and cleanup, the transport of
combustion products and heat must be predicted. Large -scale fire data was provided by the Saffire payload
within Northrup Grumman’s Cygnus vehicle after a resupply mission to t he ISS. Solid materials, including
thick thermoplastic samples were ignited to study flame spread and the effect a fire has on a vehicle.  Sensors
that measured species and temperature were placed within Cygnus. A model using Pyrosim was developed to
predict the thick fuel burn events from Saffire V by using sensor data from Cygnus. The heat transfer
coefficient between the cabin air and cargo was previously calibrated using the cotton fuel burn event, and
provided a good prediction of the temperature respon se within the vehicle during  the two thick fuel
experiments. The difference between the Saffire inlet CO2 sensor, and the forward port standoff CO 2 sensor,
which is the closest to the Saffire outlet, was used as an input into the model. This method provided an excellent
prediction of the CO2 response in the other parts of the Cygnus.

I. Introduction
spacecraft fire poses one of the greatest risks to the crew and mission success. There is a need to predict the
consequences of a fire event to reduce the risk for long -duration missions. Most flame spread testing has been
executed in 1-g or has not been large-scale1,2,3. The Saffire project has provided valuable data on large flame spread
in a microgravity environment  while acting as a test bed for novel gas species sensor technology 3,4. These series of
experiments have taken place during the end of a resupply mission to the to the International Space Station (ISS) on
Northrop Grumman’s (NG) Cygnus vehicle. After Cygnus undocks from the ISS, the experiments take place and the
data is downlinked before destructive re-entry into the earth’s atmosphere. The data collected will help designers and
modelers predict the effects of a large-scale fire in a microgravity environment in order to design a safer spacecraft.
The focus of the first three Saffire missions was on flame spread 5,6,7,8, while the Saffire IV and V expanded the
experiment to include gas release events and sensors throughout the vehicle. These sensors include diagnostic tools to
determine the temperature increase due to the fire and transport of combustion products such as CO 2. The data
collected from these far-field gas and particle sensors will provide insight into smoke plume and combustion product
transport within the vehicle9,10,11. The test events include flow visualization at the beginning and end of the campaign,
CO2 scrubs, gas releases, and burn events. Both Saffire IV and V experiments utilized six remote sensors (RS) spread
throughout Cygnus that measure temperature and product concentration, such as CO 2, as well as a Far Field Device
(FFD) that is representative of average t emperature and species concentration in the cabin.  Both Saffire experiments
had cotton based and Polymethyl (Methacrylate) (PMMA) samples as fuel for the burn events.
The focus of this paper is on the two PMMA burn events in Saffire V. Similar to previous  work12,13, the effect of
burning the PMMA samples on the vehicle temperature and combustion product spread are predicted. The geometry
and cargo configuration for Saffire V is the same that was used in previous work that explored the cotton sample in
Saffire V13. The effective convective heat transfer between the cargo and the cabin air that was calibrated in that same
work is also used in this work, which was calibrated to fit the closest RS to the Saffire outlet. The development of this
model will aid in the prediction of heat and mass transfer throughout a spacecraft after a fire.
II. Saffire Experiment
Saffire I – V have all take n place on the NG Cygnus vehicle . The experimental data collection and downlink ing
happened after the cargo from the resupply mission was unloaded onto the ISS and the trash was packed into Cygnus.
A schematic of a nearly empty  Cygnus vehicle is provided in Figure 1 before the cargo is loaded , highlighting the
Saffire payload and instruments used to collect data. The pressure vessel  is approximately 4 meters in length and 3
meters in diameter. The Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS)  recirculated flow throughout the
vehicle by taking air from the zenith side, near the (FFD), and expelling it on the nadir side where Saffire is located.

Trash and cargo were placed by the crew in a manner which constructed interconnected channels of approximately
triangular shape form, as shown in Figure 2. These channels, referred to as standoffs, transported species, smoke, and
heat from the outlet of the Saffire payload during a gas release or burn  event. The heat addition rate (HAR) and the
mass generation rate (MGR), which supply the heat and combustion products from the Saffire payload to the Cygnus
cabin, are applied to the Saffire outlet, which has a surface area of  50 cm2. The flow of gas generally went  towards
the zenith section of Cygnus from  the Saffire outlet through the forward port (FP) standoff. From there, heat and
species would either diffuse towards the other three standoffs, or flow back to the nadir section through the ECLSS
system.

Table 1 presents the experimental events completed in the Saffire V campaign.  Similar to Saffire IV, Saffire V
started with a CO 2 scrub by pulling cabin air through the FFD. Event 2 released CO 2 in the cabin to help determine
the free volume used to calculate the amount of oxygen that needed to be added between b urns. In addition to CO 2,
HF was released to study its decay in the atmosphere  since it tends to adsorb to surfaces more than other combustion
products such as CO2. A flow visualization test was performed to determine any major differences in the Saffire Flow
Unit (SFU) after all the planned burns were completed  in event 3 and 9 . Event 6 burned a one-sided PMMA, while
event 8 burned a thicker structured PMMA  with grooves and ridges of increasing dimension or separation 9. The
atmosphere for the experimental campaign was approximately 0.71 atm and 29% oxygen, with an initial temperature
of approximately 24.1°C.
III. Modeling
By using Fire Dynamics Simulator (FDS), a code developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology
(NIST), a model was constructed that predicted the effect of the PMMA burn events from Saffire V on the Cygnus
Vehicle. The focus of this large-eddy turbulent simulation code is the low-speed flow created by a compartment fire
as well as smoke and heat transfer14. PyrosimTM 15  is the graphical interface that was used to run the FDS simulations
and develop the vehicle model. A picture of this model highlighting the FFD, SFU, ECLSS system and RS locations
is provided by Figure 2. A cargo placement map and photographs provided by NG were used to construct the geometry
in Pyrosim, as was discussed in a previous paper discussing the cotton burning sample (event 5)13.

The six remote sensors served as the  main comparisons between the model and experiments.  While all remote
sensors are visible in Figure 1 without the cargo, some of the remote sensors are not visible due to the stowed material
in Figure 2. Starting with the nadir section of Cygnus where Saffire is lo cated, RS1 is positioned in the bulkhead. RS
2 -5  are in the standoffs at the midplane (zenith to nadir). Starting at the forward starboard (FS) where RS2 is located,
the others are numbered by moving counterclockwise about the other standoffs:  RS3 - forward port (FP), RS4 - aft
port (AP), and RS5 - aft starboard (AS). RS6 is in the zenith near the hatch. In both burn events, the initial RS reading
for the CO 2 was within 5% of the average, and hence thi s average was used for the initial CO 2 in the simulation of
each event. The initial temperature reading of the RS varied more than this in each event, hence the initial reading of
RS3 was used as the initial temperature of the cabin since that is the RS th at created the greatest response. The
simulation curves of the other remote sensors were then adjusted so the initial values matched.
Table 2 gives the flowrates used within Cygnus and Saffire. The flow rate of 0.028 m3/s utilized by the SFU gave
a flow of 20 cm/s over the samples, which is ramped down at certain times to extinguish the flame. In order to capture
the effect of the burn event on  the temperature of Cygnus, the HAR was calculated using the inlet and outlet
temperature of the SFU, as given by Eq. 1, and demonstrated in Figure 3 using data from event 8. This method was
utilized in previous work12,13, and is described as the sensible enthalpy rise method highlighted by the Society of Fire
Protection Engineering Handbook 16. No combustion or flame properties are modeled, hence the Saffire payload is
modeled simply as an object that takes in cool cabin air and outputs hot air and combustion products.  Any radiation
from the Saffire unit or the hot exhaust gases has been assumed to be negligib le. Other properties  of the initial
atmospheric conditions of the Cygnus vehicle are provided by Table 3. The properties of air at initial conditions (24°C)
are used to calculate the HAR since the temperature at the outlet does not exceed 45°C, and the difference in the main
combustion products (CO2 and H2O) are only in the 1000s of parts per million (ppm), making a negligible difference
in cp and density between SFU inlet and outlet conditions.

IV. Results and Discussion
The initial experimental CO2 values for burn event 6 ranged from 7,375 to 7,973 ppm, giving an average of 7,597
ppm as given by Table 3. This average value was used for the initial value for the  event 6 simulation, and then the
prediction curves were incr eased or decreased to match the initial experimental values.  Figure 7 shows the model
prediction with the experimental results for the CO2 response of the six remote sensors. An excellent agreement is
realized in each case. There is slight over prediction during the increasing part of the curve in RS 3 and a moderate
overprediction of this part of the curve in  RS6, which are in the direct path of the flow from Saffire  from the FP
standoff. While the model has solid trash and cargo lining the edges of the FP standoff, these solids are porous  in
reality, allowing for more mixing of air and combustion products on the way to RS3 and RS6, which would bring the
peak of the combustion product response down.

coefficient of 150 W/m2-K was used for the surfaces, calibrated at RS3  from the cotton burn in the previous work13.
The model initial condition used the RS3 initial condition (24.1 °C) since that was the RS that had the largest response.

However, the remote sensors ranged from 19.2 to 24.3 °C initially. Since the initial temperatures of the remote sensors
were all different, the prediction curves were adjusted so that the initial value matched that of each location, in order
to compare the increase in temperature. There was very little response besides RS3, which increased approximately 1
degree at the peak and reached steady state at about a half degree increase. There was negligible temperature difference
in the starboard standoffs (RS2 and RS5) compa red to the sensitivity of the thermocouples (+/ - 0.25°C).  This was
predicted well by the model.
Figure 9 presents the RS predictions for the structured PMMA. Excellent agreement is realized again for all RS
values, especially at steady state where both p rediction and experiment show about a  2,500 ppm increase or a  36%
increase in CO2 throughout the cabin. The model did predict a larger maximum value of CO 2 for both RS3 and RS6
which are in the direct flow path of the Saffire combustion products. It is possible that the bags of trash, which are not
perfectly rigid, partially blocked those sensors, making the CO 2 have to diffuse around the bag to get to the sensor,
allowing for a smoother response. Fluctuations in both the CO2 and temperature response of the model can be observed
by the remote sensors within the flow path and are placed in a more open part of the cabin outside of the standoffs,
such as in RS6. This is likely due to the turbulent nature of the model. The reason these fluctuations are not as
pronounced in the data is the remote sensors use a pump to suck combustion products the location to the actual sensor.
Even if it is a small area, it is averaging the concentration around the probe area, likely smoothing out the effect of
turbulence.

The predictions for RS  temperature response during the structured PMMA burn are provided in Figure 10. The
Saffire V temperatures during the structured PMMA burn, similar t o the one -sided PMMA burn case, provide very
little response outside of RS3 and RS6, which is predicted by the model. Integrating the heat addition rate from Figures
4 and 5 provide a total energy addition of 233 and 275 kJ for event 6 and event 8 respectively. Despite this moderate
difference in total heat added, the temperature response remains nearly the same for both burn events. The results from
the experiments and model , with regards to the specific spacecraft and test conditions, indicate that the fire does not
have a large effect on the vehicle temperature, except close to the fire.
An interesting phenomenon is observed by looking at the temperature profile in RS4 for both burn cases. The
experimental profile decreases at some point below the initial temperature. One theory is the ECLSS system on Cygnus
is known to cycle on an off to keep the conditions of the spacecraft within certain parameters. RS4 is in the direct path
of the ECLSS system, and on the opposite side of the hot Saffire outlet, which  is why it makes sense its profile is
dictated more by the ECLSS system as opposed to the fire. RS1, which is even closer to the ECLSS outlet, shows a
less pronounced decrease in temperature, but since it is close to the Saffire outlet, its behavior is more dictated by the
burn event.

V. Conclusions
A FDS code was developed to determine the effects of the Saffire V PMMA burn events on the Cygnus vehicle.
Measurements of CO 2 and temperature were taken throughout the vehicle, specifically at six RS locations. The
combustion product generation rates were calculated based on the scaled difference of RS3 and the SFU inlet CO 2
readings and the stoichiometry of PMMA combustion, while the heat addition rate was calculated based on the inlet
and outlet Saffire temperatures. Parts of the model cargo were hollowed out in order for the model volume to match
the volume calculated from the CO2 release events. The convective heat transfer coefficient that was calibrated at RS3
for the Saffire V cotton burn in previous work was determined to be 150 W/m2-K and gave and excellent prediction
for both Saffire V  PMMA burn events. The model did an excellent job of predicting the CO2 and a reasonable job of
predicting temperature data. The effect a spacecraft fire has on a vehicle was highlighted by the Saffire data and model,
such as product mixing and heat transfer. These results demonstrate the feasibility of predicting the imp act of a fire
on the habitability of an orbiting spacecraft.
